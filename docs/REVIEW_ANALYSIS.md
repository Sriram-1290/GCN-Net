# One-Page Review-Paper Analysis

**Project:** GBP-Net: A Graph Band-Power Network with Cross-Subject Pre-Training for EEG Motor Imagery Decoding · **Batch No.** 22 · **Guide:** Dr. Gagan Kumar

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| **Title of published review paper** | Graph Neural Network-Based EEG Classification: A Survey |
| **Authors** | Dominik Klepl, Min Wu, Fei He |
| **Journal / conference** | *IEEE Transactions on Neural Systems and Rehabilitation Engineering*, vol. 32, pp. 493–503. DOI: 10.1109/TNSRE.2024.3355750 |
| **Publication year** | 2024 |
| **Main review topic** | Graph neural network (GNN) models for classifying EEG — emotion recognition, epilepsy, motor imagery, sleep staging and neurological disorders — organised by the architectural module each paper varies rather than by application |
| **Number of papers reviewed** | 63 |
| **Databases used for searching** | Google Scholar and ScienceDirect, queried on 1 November 2022; both peer-reviewed articles and preprints included |
| **Search keywords** | ("Graph neural network" OR "Graph convolutional network") AND ("Electroencephalography" OR "EEG"), screened by title and abstract |
| **Classification of reviewed studies** | Six categories following the GNN pipeline: (1) brain-graph definition — structural/distance-based vs functional, predefined vs learnable; (2) node features — time, frequency or graph domain; (3) node-feature preprocessing; (4) graph-convolution type — spatial, spectral or spatio-temporal; (5) node pooling; (6) formation of the graph embedding |
| **Important comparison tables** | Table I: brain-graph construction methods; Table II: node-feature definitions; Table III: node-feature preprocessing; Table IV: graph-convolution layers; Table V: node pooling; Table VI: graph-embedding methods. Figure 3 gives the distribution of classification tasks, in which emotion recognition, epilepsy and motor imagery are the three most common. The survey reports this distribution but does not break any result down by task, on the stated ground that the task should have only a minor effect on architecture design |
| **Main findings** | Chebyshev spectral convolution (ChebConv) is used by most surveyed papers, often with no stated reason for choosing it over a spatial layer. Raw EEG and differential entropy are the most common node features. The least innovation appears in the choice of graph-convolution layer. Distance-based graphs are noise-free but short-range; functional graphs capture long-range coupling but are noise-sensitive |
| **Research gaps identified** | Models do not transfer to datasets with a different number of electrodes, because ChebConv and node-concatenation readouts fix the node count; transfer-learning experiments are largely absent; frequency-band and cross-frequency information is under-used; no study compares graph-convolution layer types systematically |
| **Future research directions** | A controlled comparison of spatial, spectral and attention-based graph layers; pre-training on large open EEG datasets and transferring to small ones; cross-frequency coupling as graph structure; pooling and readout that tolerate variable electrode counts |
| **Reference style used** | Numbered citations in order of first appearance, IEEE style in the published version |
| **How this paper is useful for our project** | **(1)** It reviews GCNs-Net itself, so the survey's conclusions apply directly to our baseline rather than by analogy. It confirms that ChebConv — the operator GCNs-Net uses — is the field's default and is adopted without clear justification, which motivates our controlled comparison of a graph-constrained spatial operator against an unconstrained one on identical folds. **(2)** It names a “general lack of transfer learning experiments” for EEG-GNN models and proposes pre-training on large datasets as a remedy — which is precisely what GBP-Net's cross-subject pre-training implements, and the intervention that moved our results most. **(3)** Its module-by-module taxonomy shapes the classification scheme of our own review. **(4)** It analyses *architectures* but never examines *evaluation protocols* — how samples are defined or how data are split — which is the gap our review adds. The authors also state their review is not systematic and omits explainability |
| **Main limitations of this review** | It does not analyse results by classification task, so motor imagery is never separated from emotion recognition or epilepsy, and evaluation protocol — how a sample is defined, how data are split, whether subjects overlap between train and test — is outside its scope entirely. Explainability is excluded and preprint coverage is uneven. These omissions are the opening our own review takes |
| **Why this review was selected** | **Relevance:** it is the only published review of the exact method family in our approved title, and it reviews our baseline GCNs-Net as one of its 63 articles. **Verifiability:** it is gold open access, so every entry above comes from the full text, not an abstract. **Fit:** it states its databases, query string and search date, which many narrative reviews in this field do not. Two alternatives were rejected — a systematic review of deep learning for motor imagery (Saibene *et al.*, *Neurocomputing*, 2024), closer to our task but with no accessible full text, and a transfer-learning review (Wu *et al.*, *IEEE TCDS*, 2022), which states no search strategy. Both are cited in our review paper |
